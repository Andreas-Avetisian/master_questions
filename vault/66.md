# For literature research, name and describe the five key requirements for sources. What is the reproducibility crisis?

## Five key requirements for sources

The PM1 literature-research lecture summarizes the requirements for sources under citation ability, publication, identifiability, controllability, and citation worthiness.

## 1. Citation ability

A source must be suitable for citation in scientific work. This means it must be **published**, **verifiable**, and **controllable**. A private file, personal statement, or inaccessible document is weak because readers cannot check it.

## 2. Published source

Only published sources can normally be cited. Publication makes the source available beyond the author's private context.

This does not always mean a journal article. Books, book chapters, conference papers, journal articles, and some websites can be sources. But for scientific argumentation, peer-reviewed academic sources are usually stronger than random web pages.

## 3. Identifiability

The source must be clearly identifiable. Every place where external information is used must be marked with a reference, and the bibliography must contain enough information to find the source again.

Identifiability means the reader can answer questions such as:

- Who wrote it?
- What is the title?
- Where was it published?
- When was it published?
- Where exactly can I find it?

## 4. Controllability / verifiability

Sources used in a scientific paper must be verifiable. A reader or reviewer must be able to check whether the cited source really says what the author claims.

This is also why proper page references, DOIs, URLs, publication venues, and complete bibliography entries matter. Without controllability, citations do not support traceability.

## 5. Citation worthiness

A source should be worth citing. The lecture says this is usually guaranteed by **peer review** and a corresponding publisher or venue.

Citation worthiness depends on the task. In normal literature research, peer-reviewed papers, academic books, and established venues are preferred. There are exceptions when the publication form itself is the object of research. For example, if the research investigates technical terms in computer newspapers, then computer newspapers can be valid primary sources. If the research compares tools, then tool homepages may be valid primary sources.

## Connection to systematic literature research

The MRI literature-research lecture adds the methodological side: systematic literature reviews should be transparent, reproducible, and defined before the search starts. This reduces cherry-picking and bias. Good source work therefore means not only choosing credible sources, but also documenting how sources were searched, selected, excluded, and evaluated. PRISMA helps by documenting the flow of records and exclusions.

## The reproducibility crisis

The reproducibility crisis means that many scientific results are difficult or impossible to reproduce, even though reproducibility is a cornerstone of science.

The reviewing lecture explains the problem in two places:

- Peer review is supposed to check quality, relevance, suitability for the venue, plagiarism, and reproducibility.
- In practice, reproducibility is often not doable for reviewers.

Reasons include:

- too much time would be required
- reproduction would be too expensive
- data is not available
- details about setup, methodology, parameters, or implementation are missing

This creates a serious problem: some published results may look good and may even be accepted through peer review, but still cannot be independently reproduced. Then other researchers cannot reliably check correctness or build upon the results.

## Short summary

Good literature sources should be **citable, published, identifiable, controllable/verifiable, and citation-worthy**. In practice this means complete references, accessible sources, peer-reviewed or otherwise justified material, and enough information for readers to check the claim. The **reproducibility crisis** is the problem that scientific results are often hard to reproduce because data, setup details, time, money, or methodological transparency are missing. This weakens trust in published results and makes systematic, well-documented source work essential.
