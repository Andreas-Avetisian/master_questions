# Explain the following terms: Random experiment, target variable, influencing variable and confounding factors.

## Random experiment

A **random experiment** is a real process:

- that can have different possible results
- where exactly one result occurs in a concrete run
- whose outcome is uncertain before the run
- which can, in principle, be repeated under the same conditions

Simple examples are a coin toss, dice roll, or lottery drawing. The lecture also gives applied examples such as quality control, analysis results, and drug approval studies.

In experimental design, the experiment is a structured "question to nature". The question should be formulated precisely enough that the result can be evaluated in a way others can understand.

Example: instead of asking vaguely whether television is harmful for children, formulate a precise question such as whether five-year-old children from a defined population who watch more than five action films per week show more aggressive behavior than comparable children who do not watch television.

## Target variable

A **target variable** is the random variable observed in an experiment about which we want to make a statement.

Other names:

- dependent variable
- endpoint
- response variable

Examples:

- relapse rate in a medical study
- strength of a material
- reaction time in an alcohol-consumption experiment
- health improvement after a drug treatment

The target variable is what the experiment tries to explain, predict, improve, or compare.

## Influencing variable

An **influencing variable** is a variable that influences the value of a target variable.

Other name:

- independent variable

The lecture distinguishes two kinds:

1. **Factors**

   These are influencing variables that can be recorded, observed, or varied in the experiment.

   Examples: treatment type, process temperature, alcohol consumption, dosage, experimental condition.

2. **Experimental errors / unobserved influencing variables**

   These are remaining influencing variables that cannot be observed, varied, or included because there are too many of them or because technical reasons make this impossible. They are summarized as random error.

Important: whether a variable is a target variable or influencing variable depends on the experimental arrangement. Alcohol consumption could be an influencing variable in a study on reaction time, but it could also be the target variable in a study on the effect of stimulus withdrawal on drinking behavior.

## Confounding factors

**Confounding factors** are factors that influence both:

- the independent / influencing variable, and
- the dependent / target variable.

They cannot simply be manipulated away, and they can make effects overlap, disappear, or appear stronger than they really are. This creates a detection and interpretation problem.

Typical confounding factors from the lecture include:

- environmental influences such as temperature or pressure
- time influences, for example measurements at different times
- maturation of the experimental unit, such as learning or ageing effects
- changes in measuring instruments, such as wear
- experiment-leader effects, such as fatigue or increased sensitivity over time
- changes of test manager

Confounding is dangerous because it can make us attribute an effect to the wrong cause.

## Short summary

A **random experiment** is a repeatable process with uncertain outcome. The **target variable** is the dependent variable or endpoint we want to explain. An **influencing variable** is an independent variable that affects the target variable; if it can be controlled or observed, it is a factor. **Confounding factors** influence both the independent and dependent variables and therefore can distort causal interpretation.
