# Name 5 structural elements of research proposals as discussed in the lecture. What is the typical structure of a workpackage?

## Structural elements of research proposals

The lecture notes that proposal structures can be named differently depending on the funding program, but typical parts include:

1. **Abstract**: short summary that sells the project and gives the first impression.
2. **State of the art**: shows the current knowledge, the research gap, and why the project is novel.
3. **Targets**: the main target and sub-targets or research questions.
4. **Methodology**: explains how the project will reach its goals.
5. **Risks and countermeasures**: identifies relevant project risks and how they will be handled.
6. **Workpackages and work plan**: structures the work into manageable parts.
7. **Partner and consortium description**: explains who is involved and why this team is suitable.
8. **Personal description**: shows the relevant competence and track record of people involved.
9. **Gender, diversity and sustainability**: addresses non-technical requirements and expected impacts.
10. **Dissemination and exploitation**: explains how results will be communicated and used.
11. **Call-specific aspects**: addresses special requirements of the funding call.

If only five are required, a strong exam answer is: **abstract, state of the art, targets, methodology, and workpackages/work plan**.

## Structural elements in the work plan

The lecture also discusses structural elements inside larger projects:

- **Sub-projects**: used in very large structural programs.
- **Modules / segments**: used in large projects such as research centers or labs.
- **Workpackages**: the main structure in most projects and often the typical budgeting level.
- **Tasks**: often the smallest work items.
- **Deliverables**: concrete outputs, usually assigned to a workpackage.
- **Milestones**: important checkpoints with due dates.
- **Timelines**: often built from workpackage and milestone dates.

## Typical structure of a workpackage

A workpackage is often the most fundamental structural element of the project. The exact requirements differ by program, but a typical workpackage contains:

1. **Tasks**

   The actual work items inside the workpackage. Tasks are often the smallest described units, even if they are not always separately budgeted.

2. **Deliverables**

   The concrete outputs of the workpackage, for example reports, prototypes, datasets, software, documentation, or demonstrators. A deliverable normally has a type or form, a content description, and often a due date.

3. **Milestones**

   Decision points or checkpoints that show whether important progress has been reached. A milestone always has a due date and a description, and sometimes a responsible partner.

4. **Time interval**

   The planned start and end of the workpackage. This links the workpackage to the overall timeline.

5. **Involved partners**

   The organizations or people contributing to the workpackage. Often there is also a responsible partner or workpackage lead.

6. **Risks**

   Risks can be described on workpackage level or elevated to an overarching risk section. They should be specific to the project.

7. **Budget / effort**

   Workpackages are often the level where bottom-up budgeting is done, even if the exact budget representation depends on the funding program.

## Short summary

Typical proposal elements are abstract, state of the art, targets, methodology, risks, workpackages/work plan, consortium description, dissemination/exploitation, and call-specific sections. In the work plan, the main elements are workpackages, tasks, deliverables, milestones, timelines, partners, and risks. A typical workpackage contains tasks, deliverables, milestones, time interval, involved partners, responsible partner, risks, and often effort or budget information.
