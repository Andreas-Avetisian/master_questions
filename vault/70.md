# Why are one-pagers relevant? What should they contain? What should not be in a one-pager in general?

## Why one-pagers are relevant

A one-pager is a short project story or project sketch. It is relevant because it forces the project idea into a form that can be understood, discussed, challenged, and shared early.

The WRP lecture mentions one-pagers in several practical contexts:

- Write a one-pager and reach out to potential partners.
- A one-pager can be a good source for the abstract.
- It is useful for gathering new partners.
- It helps assess what additional expertise is required.
- It supports resource estimation.
- It helps check whether the proposal matches the call, both topic-wise and budget-wise.
- It allows more directed literature research.
- It fixes the goals between partners and puts everyone on the same page.

So the one-pager is not just a summary at the end. It is an early coordination and selling tool.

## What a one-pager should contain

A good one-pager should contain the core of the project story.

Important contents are:

1. **Problem and scope**

   What is the problem? What part of the problem will this project actually address?

2. **Main target**

   What do we want to achieve? This should be short, concrete, and understandable.

3. **Motivation / sale**

   Why is this worth doing? Why should partners, reviewers, or funders care?

4. **Solution idea**

   What is the basic approach? The one-pager should show the logic of the solution without becoming a full methodology chapter.

5. **Research questions and workpackages**

   What are the important steps to the solution? These can later become research questions, workpackages, and milestones.

6. **Expected result after the project**

   What should exist afterwards? This includes expected deliverables and integration depth.

7. **Challenges and risks at high level**

   What are the main hard parts? The one-pager should show awareness of risks, but not contain a fine-grained risk register.

8. **Required partners and expertise**

   Who is needed to make the project credible? This is especially important when the one-pager is used for partner acquisition.

9. **Fit to funding scheme or call**

   The one-pager should make visible why this idea fits the call and whether the likely budget and scope are realistic.

10. **One or two useful figures**

   The lecture recommends using one or two figures if possible and suitable. A diagram can make the story easier to understand.

## What should not be in a one-pager

The lecture is explicit that the story should be short and concise.

Do not include:

- too many details
- formulas
- unnecessary specifics
- fine-tuned risk analysis
- exact amount of money required
- a full literature review
- excessive background information
- disconnected partner wish lists
- all technical implementation details

The one-pager should also avoid becoming a **Frankenstein project**, where unrelated partner interests are glued together without one coherent story.

## Quality criteria

A good one-pager should pass the same tests as a good project story:

- Can a non-expert understand the research idea from it?
- Can the important parts be visualized?
- Will the partners you need understand it?

If the answer is no, the one-pager is too vague, too technical, or not structured enough.

## Short summary

One-pagers are relevant because they make the project idea shareable early: they help find partners, estimate resources, check call fit, direct literature research, and align goals. They should contain the problem, target, motivation, solution idea, main steps, expected results, high-level risks, needed partners, and call fit, ideally with one or two figures. They should not contain excessive detail, formulas, exact budgets, fine-grained risk analysis, or a full literature review.
