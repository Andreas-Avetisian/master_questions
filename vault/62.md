# Name and explain the 5 key questions the "story" of a project needs to answer. What are important steps in the story development?

## The five key questions of a project story

The lecture treats the project **story** as the short, coherent explanation of why a project should exist and how it will work. It needs to answer five questions.

1. **What do we want to achieve?**

   This is the **main target** of the project. It should be clear, short, and concrete enough that reviewers and partners understand the intended result.

2. **Why do we want to achieve it?**

   This is the **sale** of the project: the motivation, relevance, need, or impact. It explains why the problem matters and why the project deserves money and attention.

3. **What are the important steps to the solution?**

   These become the **research questions** and **workpackages**. The story should show the logical path from problem to solution, not just list activities.

4. **What are the challenges and risks?**

   These shape the **project structure**. A good story does not pretend that everything is trivial; it identifies the hard parts and shows how the project will handle them.

5. **What shall be there after the project?**

   This defines the expected end state: **integration depth** and **deliverables**. It should be clear what exists after the project that did not exist before.

## Why a good story helps

A good story helps define:

- the problem and actual project scope
- workpackages and milestones
- the integration level and deliverables
- the required partners
- the correct funding scheme
- risks and open problems
- ethical, gender, diversity, sustainability, and other non-technical impacts

It also helps sell the problem, the proposed solution, and the consortium as the right team.

## Important steps in story development

**1. Write it as early as possible.**

The lecture says to write the story early because it helps gather partners, estimate required expertise and resources, check whether the project matches the call, direct the literature research, and put all partners on the same page.

**2. Start with idea gathering.**

Ideas may be collected alone or in a group, but they must later be consolidated. A pile of ideas is not yet a story.

**3. Use workshops.**

Workshops support brainstorming and consolidation. Group sizes can vary, and the lecture allows open brainstorming but warns against being crazy only for the sake of it.

**4. Discuss with partners.**

Partner discussions are important because the story must be shared. Quiet partners should be supported so that the final story is not dominated by only the loudest people.

**5. Use storyboards.**

Storyboards help keep track of the story. They can be graphical or document-based, should be accessible to everyone, and changes should be clear and transparent.

**6. Use a devil's advocate.**

Someone should actively challenge the story. This criticism is important, but it should be fair and target the proposal, not people personally.

**7. Draft early and iterate.**

An early draft fixes the current shared understanding. Later it can be rewritten based on inputs. The story is allowed to change when new partners, related work, interests, scope, or budget constraints change.

**8. Keep it short and simple.**

The lecture explicitly recommends **KISS**: keep it short and simple. The story should not contain all details.

**9. Write it down clearly.**

The written story should be short and concise and can use one or two figures if useful.

Do not include:

- too many details
- formulas
- unnecessary specifics
- fine-tuned risk analysis
- exact amount of money required

## Quality criteria

A good project story passes three simple tests:

- Can you explain the research to a non-expert using only the story?
- Can you visualize the important parts graphically?
- Will the partners you need understand it?

## Short summary

The project story answers: **what do we want to achieve, why, by which steps, with which risks, and what exists afterwards?** Story development should start early, use brainstorming and consolidation, involve partner discussions, use storyboards, include devil's-advocate criticism, produce an early draft, and iterate. The final story should be concise, understandable, visual where useful, and free of unnecessary technical detail.
